Money

What music distribution actually costs in 2026

Figures current as of July 2026 · Updated when pricing changes

Every distributor advertises a headline price and most of them say "keep 100% of your royalties" somewhere on the page. Both things can be true while you still end up worse off, because the headline price and the royalty split are not the only two variables. There are three:

  1. The fee: annual, or one-time per release.
  2. The streaming cut: what they take from Spotify, Apple, and friends.
  3. The social cut: what they take from TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube, which is frequently not the same number, and is frequently not on the pricing page.

That third one is where independent rappers get quietly taxed, because short-form is where rap actually breaks.

Total cost means fees paid plus royalties taken, over three years. CD Baby's figure is a best case, because its social cut is unpublished and applied here at the same 9% as distribution.

DistroKid is not winning here because it is cheap. Its fee is identical to TuneCore's. It wins because it takes no percentage, and once you earn anything at all from short-form, the percentage is the entire story.

The sticker price is noise. The cut is the cost.

The two break-even numbers that actually decide it

Forget comparing $24.99 to $24.99. DistroKid's entry plan and TuneCore's are the same price to the cent, so the sticker is noise. What separates these services is whether a percentage cut costs you more than a flat fee, and that depends entirely on how much you earn.

Break-even #1: the social cut, at about $125/year

TuneCore takes 20% of TikTok, Instagram, Facebook and YouTube earnings, and it does that on the paid plans too, not just a free tier. DistroKid's entry plan is $24.99/year with no percentage.

20% of $125 = $25. So once you earn more than about $125 a year from short-form, TuneCore's social cut on its own costs you more than DistroKid's entire annual fee, and that's before you have paid TuneCore's own $24.99.

$125 a year is not a lot. One song used across a few thousand TikToks clears it. If your music has any short-form life at all, the percentage is the expensive part and the subscription price is a rounding error.

Break-even #2: the royalty cut, at about $280/year

CD Baby's model is genuinely different and genuinely good for one situation: you pay once, the release never expires, and you never renew. The trade is 9% of everything, forever.

9% of $280 = $25. Below about $280/year in total royalties, CD Baby's cut is smaller than DistroKid's annual fee. Above it you are paying more every year, permanently.

So which one

The mistake that costs more than any of this

Letting a subscription lapse. On subscription distributors, your catalogue comes down when you stop paying, and re-uploading is not a neutral act. You can lose the Spotify URI, the playlist placements attached to it, and the play counts that made the song look alive. A declined card in an inbox you don't check can undo two years of slow growth for $24.99.

Whatever you choose: put the renewal on a card that won't expire, and set a calendar reminder two weeks before the renewal date. That reminder is worth more than picking the optimal distributor.
