Spoilers don't work on music

4 August 2026 · A follow-up to The layup still counts. Everything here is sourced, and what didn't hold up is listed at the bottom next to what did.

Last week I wrote that Meek Mill going back to his own most famous beat wasn't laziness, because inducing hype is a real skill and he happens to be the outlier at it. I thought I was finished with that song. I wasn't, because I kept playing it, and playing it turned up something I can't explain with the argument I just made.

Here's what I actually did the first time. The thing everybody remembers about the 2012 Dreams and Nightmares (Intro) is the turn: it opens restrained and reflective, and then it detonates. So while the sequel was playing, I made a prediction. I decided, quite confidently, that there was no chance he was going to pull that off twice. You don't get to recreate that. It's a one-time trick and everyone already knows the trick.

He pulled it off. The turn came and it landed on me exactly the way it wasn't supposed to, and I couldn't tell you how he did it, because I was actively braced against it.

That alone is just me being wrong, which is fine. The part that actually bothered me came after. For the past few days I've had the song on constantly, and every single time, I am waiting for it. I know where it is. I know precisely what happens. And the waiting hasn't gone stale, and the payoff hasn't flattened out.

Why that should be impossible

Take the ordinary explanation for why a beat switch works: it's a violation of expectation. You're set up to expect one thing, you get another, and the jolt is the pleasure.

If that's the mechanism, then knowing is fatal. The second listen should be weaker than the first, the tenth weaker than the second, and by day four of playing something on repeat the moment should be completely inert. That's not a subtle prediction, it's the direct consequence of the theory. It is also obviously false, and not just for me. Nobody has ever stopped a song before the drop because they'd already heard the drop.

I assumed this was a hole in my own thinking. It isn't. It's a hole in the theory, it's well known, and it has a name.

It's called the rehearing problem. Expectation-based accounts of musical emotion predict that a piece should lose its emotional impact as a listener grows familiar with it. Experience says otherwise. The gap between those two is a recognised problem in the field, not a stray observation, and the fix that music psychology settled on is stranger than the problem.

The fix: part of you doesn't get the memo

The resolution the field converged on is that the mind handling music is modular. The fast system that parses incoming sound and reacts to it is not the same system that holds your knowledge of the song, and crucially, the first one does not take instruction from the second.

David Huron's framework splits the response to an expected event into several stages that fire at different speeds. The immediate reaction is reflexive, subcortical and fast enough that it's already happening before conscious appraisal arrives to say "relax, you own this record." Appraisal comes second and can only comment after the fact.

Which means the honest description of what's happening to me is that I have two things going on at once. The part of me that knows the song has the timestamp memorised. The part of me that flinches has learned nothing in four days and is not capable of learning it. Knowing the twist and being defended against it turn out to be completely different propositions, and only the first one is available to me.

The waiting is not the boring part

The other thing I had backwards was treating the run-up as dead time before the good bit.

Using PET and fMRI on the same listeners, Salimpoor and colleagues found dopamine release in the striatum around peak emotional moments in music, and, more usefully for this argument, a split: the caudate was more involved during the anticipation, and the nucleus accumbens during the peak itself. Two anatomically distinct events, not one reward with a queue in front of it.

So "I keep waiting for the flip" isn't me describing patience. The waiting is itself a rewarded state, which is why it doesn't feel like a cost, and why a moment you can see coming from thirty seconds out can be better than a genuine surprise rather than worse. A surprise only pays once, at the moment of impact. A known payoff pays across the whole approach.

Familiarity turns out to help in general, too. In an fMRI study that deliberately separated familiarity from preference, familiar music produced significantly more activity in emotion-related limbic and paralimbic regions and in the reward circuitry than unfamiliar music did, and that held regardless of whether the listener actually liked the track.

What this does to the first take

This is where it stops being trivia, because it repairs the weakest joint in the argument I made last week.

My defence of reusing the beat was essentially sentimental: he's so good at this that I'll allow it. But if the effect ran on surprise, reusing the beat really would be spending down a finite resource, and my instinct to call it a layup would have been right. You can't shock the same person with the same thing twice.

The mechanism was never surprise. It's a structure that keeps working on the fast system after the slow system has fully memorised it. That kind of moment isn't consumed by being heard, which means going back to it isn't withdrawal from an account. It's re-running a machine that was built to survive being known.

And that reframes what the difficult part of the job is. Building a surprise is comparatively easy and it degrades on contact. Building a moment that still works on the ten-thousandth play, on people who can hum the turn, is the rarer skill, and it's the one that gets a song conscripted by a football team five years later. The reason he could go back to it is the same reason it was worth going back to.

Where I overstated it

I want to be careful, because I went into this wanting the answer to be "it hits forever" and that is not what the evidence says.

Habituation is real and measurable. In a study of 58 people exposed repeatedly to chill-evoking stimuli, the likelihood of getting chills fell with repeated exposure. It didn't hold steady, it dropped. What did hold up is more interesting: among the people who still got them, the chills got more intense and lasted longer across successive exposures.

So the accurate version of my four days isn't "it lands every time." It's that it will land less often as the weeks go by, and harder when it does. Which, annoyingly, is a better description of what I'm experiencing than the one I started with.

Spoilers don't work on music. That's not a figure of speech about how much I like the song, it's a structural fact about which part of you is listening, and it is the actual reason the layup counts.

The receipts

What the evidence supports, what's shaky, and what I believed going in that turned out not to be backed. No Cold Take runs without this block.

Holds up

  • The rehearing problem is a recognised problem, not my invention. Expectation-based theories predict that music diminishes in emotional impact as familiarity grows, a prediction treated in the literature as contrary to experience, and the modular account (a fast parser separate from the long-term memory apparatus) is the standard response to it. — Huron, Sweet Anticipation (MIT Press, 2006); Music Theory Online review
  • Expectation responses fire in stages at different speeds. Huron's ITPRA model separates fast reflexive reaction from slower conscious appraisal, which is what allows a known event to still produce an involuntary response. — Sweet Anticipation
  • Anticipation and peak are anatomically distinct. PET plus fMRI in the same listeners found striatal dopamine release at peak emotional arousal, with the caudate more involved during anticipation and the nucleus accumbens during the peak experience. — Salimpoor et al., Nature Neuroscience 14, 257–262 (2011)
  • Familiar music engages reward circuitry more than unfamiliar music. With familiarity and preference separated by design, familiar tracks produced significantly greater limbic, paralimbic and reward-circuit activation, independent of liking. — Pereira et al., PLoS ONE 6(11): e27241 (2011)
  • Pleasure tracks an interaction of uncertainty and surprise, not surprise alone. Modelling roughly 80,000 chords from Billboard pop, pleasure was highest either when a chord was surprising in a low-uncertainty context or unsurprising in a high-uncertainty one, with amygdala, hippocampus and auditory cortex reflecting the interaction. — Cheung et al., Current Biology (2019)
  • Repeated exposure reduces how often chills happen. 58 participants, six pre-validated chill-evoking stimuli, counterbalanced order: likelihood of chills fell across repetitions while intensity and duration rose among those still responding. — Schoeller et al., PLOS ONE (2025)

Shaky, so don't build on it

  • I can't reconcile my own description of the sequel's structure. I hear a turn in the back half of Nightmares To Dreams. Published write-ups describe the sequel as inverting the original, putting the more menacing half first. Nobody has published a timestamped breakdown, so either I'm hearing the reversal as a turn or the write-ups are compressing something. Treat my account of where the flip sits as one listener's, not established. — Complex, stupidDOPE
  • None of this research is about rap. The chord-based modelling is trained on Billboard pop harmony, and a rap beat switch is a change in drums, texture and energy rather than a harmonic event. The general finding may well transfer; the specific model almost certainly doesn't.
  • Chills and "the flip landing" are not the same measurement. The habituation study measures frisson, which is a specific physiological event. What I'm describing might be a milder relative of it. I'm borrowing the finding across a gap.
  • Four days is not a longitudinal study. My whole starting observation is one person, one song, one week, reported by the person with the conclusion already in mind.

Didn't survive checking

  • "It still hits every single time." This is what I set out to argue and it's wrong. Repeated exposure significantly decreased the likelihood of chills in the one study I found that tested it directly. The effect does wear out; I've simply not reached the end of it yet in four days. The survivable version of my claim is narrower and better: it fires less often over time, and more intensely when it does fire. — PLOS ONE, 2025
  • "Surprise is what makes a beat switch work." I'd been carrying this around as obvious. On the evidence, surprise on its own is a poor predictor of musical pleasure — it only predicts well in interaction with how uncertain the surrounding context is. A beat switch you can see coming isn't a broken surprise, it's a different mechanism that I'd been misfiling for years. — Current Biology, 2019
